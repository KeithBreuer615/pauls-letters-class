Lesson 17Romans, Part 3

Mercy for Jews and Gentiles

God’s sovereign mercy humbles both Jews and Gentiles and preserves hope for Israel within his saving purpose.

Series locationLesson 17 of 24
Primary textsRomans 9:1–8; 10:9–15; 11:1–6, 17–32

Follow Paul’s argument

  1. Romans 9:1–29: God’s word has not failed; his promise advances through his call and mercy.
  2. Romans 9:30–10:21: Israel stumbled over Christ, yet the gospel is near and offered to everyone who believes.
  3. Romans 11:1–24: God preserves a Jewish remnant and warns grafted-in Gentiles not to boast.
  4. Romans 11:25–36: Israel’s hardening is partial; God’s mercy moves history toward hope and worship.

Central line

Mercy leaves no room for entitlement, despair, or boasting—only faith, mission, hope, and worship.

Romans 9 and 10 belong together

Romans 9 removes entitlement: God’s promise depends on his call and mercy, not ancestry or effort. Romans 10 removes fatalism: the gospel is near, everyone who calls on the Lord will be saved, and people must be sent so others may hear and believe. Paul holds God’s sovereignty and human responsibility together.

One olive tree

God has not rejected Israel. He preserves a remnant by grace. Gentile believers are wild branches grafted in through faith; they do not support the root, but the root supports them. Some natural branches were broken off through unbelief, and God is able to graft them in again. The church’s posture is gratitude, prayer, witness, and hope rather than superiority.

What does “all Israel will be saved” mean?

Faithful interpreters differ: the phrase may point to a future broad turning of ethnic Israel to Christ, the full number of Jewish believers gathered across history, or the complete people of God in Christ. The immediate contrast with the Gentiles makes ethnic Israel a natural reading, but Paul’s clear pastoral point is that salvation comes through Christ, Gentile believers must not boast, and God has not finished with Israel.

Contemporary significance

The Gospel Then and Now

Church pressure then

Israel’s unbelief raised a painful question

Paul grieved over fellow Israelites who had received the promises, while many had not believed in Jesus as Messiah.

Church temptation now

Grace can become a reason for pride

Christians may treat election as a badge, speak contemptuously about Jews, or confuse confidence in God’s mercy with certainty about every detail of his plan.

Gospel-shaped church

One mercy creates humble witness

Jews and Gentiles alike are saved through Christ by mercy, so the church proclaims the gospel, rejects superiority, and hopes in God’s faithfulness.

Discuss

  1. Why does Paul begin this difficult teaching with grief and prayer rather than detached argument?
  2. How does the olive tree confront both antisemitism and ordinary Christian pride?
  3. How do Romans 9 and 10 prevent both entitlement and fatalism?
  4. Why is worship, rather than a solved theological system, Paul’s conclusion in Romans 11:33–36?
