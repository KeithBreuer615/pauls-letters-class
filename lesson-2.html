Lesson 2

Saul Before Paul

When religious certainty is confronted by the risen Christ.

Primary textActs 7:54–9:19
Paul interpretsGalatians 1:11–24; Philippians 3:4–11

Follow the story

Saul’s religious credentials and zeal made him certain he was serving God, even while he opposed Christ and attacked Christ’s church. By grace, Jesus confronted him, overturned his confidence, and brought him into the church he tried to destroy.

Acts

What happened?

Saul approved violence, opposed the church, met the risen Jesus, and entered Damascus blind.

Galatians

Who acted?

God set Paul apart, called him by grace, revealed his Son, and commissioned him.

Philippians

What changed?

Former gains became loss; knowing Christ became Paul’s surpassing treasure.

The church

Who received him?

Ananias trusted Christ’s authority and addressed the former persecutor as “Brother Saul.”

Remember

Before Saul knew he needed grace, Stephen was praying for grace to be shown to his persecutors—including Saul.

Contemporary significance

The Gospel Then and Now

Saul’s heritage, knowledge, discipline, and zeal were not worthless gifts. The danger was making them grounds for confidence and using zeal to oppose the very people Christ claimed as his own.

Church problem then

Confidence without Christ

Saul’s religious résumé fed confidence in the flesh, and his zeal for defending what he believed was right became destructive toward Christ’s church.

Church temptation now

Good gifts become idols

Churches can turn doctrine, tradition, denominational history, moral reputation, political identity, years of service, or ministry success into grounds for superiority and exclusion.

Gospel-shaped church

Grace redirects confidence

Christ gives his people their identity and righteousness. Knowledge, tradition, experience, and zeal are then received as gifts for serving the church—not credentials for ranking its members.

Discuss

  1. What warning does this story give churches about religious certainty that refuses to listen?
  2. What did obedience require Ananias to believe about Jesus—and about Saul?
  3. What good religious gifts can churches turn into grounds for confidence or superiority?
  4. How can a church value those gifts without placing its confidence in them?
